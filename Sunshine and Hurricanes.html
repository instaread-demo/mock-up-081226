iPhone & Android Parental Controls: Full 2026 Setup Guide

Written by Kira Lewis

Quick answer: On iPhone, go to Settings → Screen Time → Turn On Screen Time, then set up Family Sharing so you can manage it remotely. On Android, install the free Google Family Link app, create a supervised account for your child, and link their device to yours. Both take about 15–20 minutes and are completely free. The rest of this guide walks through exactly what to turn on, in what order, and the specific bypass tricks kids use to get around each one.ls and here’s how.

When we got our son his first iPhone, I honestly thought we’d be fine. My husband and I are both tech-savvy and this is supposed to be our thing. But I still worried we wouldn’t be able to build a strong enough safety net to keep him from stumbling onto something dangerous or just not age-appropriate. And if I felt that uncertain with a tech background, I can’t imagine how overwhelmed parents without that experience must feel trying to figure this out.

Here’s the humbling part: even with everything we knew, our son still found a way around our filtering. He’d get on gaming apps late at night, well after we thought he was asleep. It wasn’t some elaborate hack. It was just a kid who had more time and motivation to poke at the settings than we had to stay one step ahead of him.

That’s really what pushed me to actually understand every layer of Screen Time and Family Link, instead of just turning them on once and assuming we were covered. This guide is everything I wish I’d known from day one, including the workarounds kids actually use, because ours found at least one of them.

Why Built-In Controls Are Worth Setting Up First

Before you buy a third-party app or a parental-control router, use what’s already on the phone. Apple’s Screen Time and Google’s Family Link are both free, built directly into the operating system, and — as of iOS 26 and current Android versions — genuinely capable. They won’t catch everything (more on that below), but they’re the foundation everything else builds on top of. If you’ve read our post on the most dangerous apps for kids, this is the “how to actually do something about it” follow-up.

Setting Up iPhone Parental Controls (iOS 26)

Step 1: Set a Screen Time Passcode

This is the step almost every parent skips, and it’s the reason their controls don’t hold. Without a separate passcode, your child can just turn Screen Time off.

  1. On your child’s iPhone: Settings → Screen Time → Use Screen Time Passcode
  2. Set a 4-digit code that’s different from the device’s unlock passcode
  3. Under Content & Privacy Restrictions → Allow Changes → Account Changes, set to Don’t Allow — this stops your child from disabling the whole system later

Step 2: Set Up Family Sharing (Manage It Remotely)

Once this is done, you never need to touch your child’s phone again to adjust settings.

  1. On your phone: Settings → [Your Name] → Family Sharing → Add Member
  2. Choose Create Child Account if your child doesn’t have an Apple ID yet (required for kids under 13)
  3. Once added, go to Settings → Screen Time → [Child’s Name] on your own phone — you now control everything remotely and can see usage reports without touching their device

Step 3: Turn On Content & Privacy Restrictions

This is where the real protection lives.

  • App Store, Media & Games ratings — restrict by age rating (4+, 9+, 12+, 17+)
  • Web Content — under Content & Privacy Restrictions → Web Content, choose Limit Adult Websites
  • In-app purchases — set to Don’t Allow to stop surprise charges (this alone prevents one of the most common “my kid spent $500 on a game” stories)
  • Ask to Buy — every download, even free ones, requires your approval first
  • New in iOS 26: Ask to Browse lets you require approval before your child visits any new website in Safari, not just blocked categories

Step 4: Turn On Communication Safety

This scans photos and videos sent or received in Messages, FaceTime, and (new in iOS 26) system-wide for nudity, and blurs it before your child sees it — with an option to notify you for younger kids. It’s on by default for any Apple Account under 18 as of iOS 26, but it’s worth confirming it’s active: Settings → Screen Time → Communication Safety.

Step 5: Set App Limits and Downtime

  • App Limits — cap time on categories like Social Networking or Games
  • Downtime — block all apps except ones you specifically allow (great for bedtime and homework hours)
  • One catch: kids can tap “Ignore Limit” and request more time. Without the Screen Time passcode from Step 1, they can just grant themselves the extension.

The Bypass Tricks Worth Knowing About

Kids are resourceful, and a few workarounds show up again and again:

  • Private Wi-Fi Address (rotating MAC address): iPhones disguise themselves with a randomized network address by default, which can make router-based filtering (Circle, some Netgear/Eero setups) lose track of the device. If you’re also using router-level controls, this is worth locking down on your child’s phone.
  • Private Relay: If you use DNS-based filtering at the router level, Apple’s Private Relay (under iCloud settings) can route around it. Turn it off on managed child accounts if you rely on network-level filtering.
  • Screen Time access permissions: As of iOS 26.4, apps that rely on Screen Time permissions to function now require the Screen Time passcode (not just Face ID) to be disabled — this closed a common workaround where kids could quietly turn off a monitoring app’s permissions.

Setting Up Android Parental Controls (Google Family Link)

Family Link is Google’s free parental control app, and it’s the Android equivalent of Screen Time — it just lives in a separate app instead of Settings.

Step 1: Install Family Link on Both Devices

Download Google Family Link from the App Store or Google Play on your own phone, and make sure it’s set up on your child’s Android device (6.0+) or Chromebook.

Step 2: Create or Supervise Your Child’s Account

On the child’s device: Settings → Google → Parental controls → Get started, then enter the code shown in your Family Link app to link the two

If your child doesn’t have a Google Account yet, create one through Family Link — this is required for kids under 13

If they already have an account, choose Supervise account instead

Step 3: Set Screen Time and Downtime

In the Family Link app, tap your child’s name → Controls → Screen time:

  • Daily limit — set a max number of hours; the device locks to approved apps only once time is up
  • Bedtime schedule — automatically restrict the device during set hours
  • App-specific timers — limit individual apps (like TikTok or YouTube) separately from overall screen time

Step 4: Control App Downloads and Purchases

Under Controls → Google Play:

  • Require approval for — choose whether every download, paid content, or just in-app purchases need your sign-off
  • Content restrictions — set age-based limits for apps, games, movies, and books

Step 5: Turn On Location Sharing

Under Location in the Family Link app, you can see your child’s Android or Chromebook location whenever the device is online — useful for peace of mind, and worth being upfront with your child about, rather than presenting it as secret surveillance.

What Family Link Can’t Do

Worth knowing going in: Family Link doesn’t read texts, DMs, or chat content, and it can’t restrict what happens inside non-Google apps — Instagram, Snapchat, Discord, etc. all need their own in-app settings reviewed separately. For that layer, a dedicated monitoring app like the ones we cover in our guide to protecting kids on dangerous apps fills the gap.

iPhone vs. Android: Which Has Stronger Built-In Controls?

Neither is flatly “better” — they’re built differently:

For most families, the honest answer is: set up whichever one matches your child’s device, and treat it as your baseline — not your entire safety plan.

Age-by-Age: What Actually Matters

  • Under 10: Full Downtime outside of approved hours, Ask to Buy/all Play Store purchases require approval, no unsupervised social media or messaging apps at all.
  • Tweens (10–13): Start layering in supervised messaging apps if any, keep app-category time limits in place, review the App Store/Play Store download history together weekly rather than silently monitoring.
  • Teens (14+): Shift from blocking to visibility — daily limits and bedtime downtime still matter, but this is the age to have direct conversations (see our Cell Phone Rules for Tweens and Teens contract) rather than relying purely on restrictions they’re increasingly capable of working around.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need a third-party parental control app if I already set up Screen Time or Family Link? Not necessarily, but built-in controls have real gaps — neither can see inside messaging apps or flag concerning content in real time the way apps like Bark can. For younger kids on a tightly managed phone, built-in controls alone are often enough. For teens with broader access, most parents pair the built-in tools with one dedicated monitoring app.

Can my child turn off Screen Time or Family Link without me knowing? Not if it’s set up correctly. The Screen Time passcode (separate from the device passcode) and Family Link’s account-level supervision are both designed so the child can’t remove them without your credentials. The common failure point is skipping the passcode step entirely.

What’s the single most important setting to turn on first? The Screen Time passcode (iPhone) or account supervision (Android). Everything else you configure only holds if this is locked down first — it’s the one step that prevents a child from simply undoing your other settings.

Do these controls work on apps like Snapchat, Instagram, or Discord? Only partially. You can restrict time spent in those apps and block downloading them in the first place, but neither Screen Time nor Family Link can see or moderate what happens inside a conversation. That’s the gap third-party monitoring apps are built to fill.
