When Pride Becomes Performative: The Hidden Cost Of Workplace Tokenism

By IMAN M'FAH TRAORE

July 22 2026, Published 8:00 a.m. ET

Pride Month happens every June. And every June, queer folks take to the streets to celebrate, march, and advocate. During that time, companies attempt to join the conversation by turning their logos rainbow, having Pride-themed panels, and even showcasing their queer employees across social media. Some of these efforts are meaningful, but, unfortunately, many are not. The difference often comes down to whether inclusion exists beyond the month of June. 

Nearly half of queer employees report experiencing unfair treatment at work at some point in their lives because of their sexual orientation or gender identity. At the same time, many queer professionals continue to alter how they present themselves, communicate, or show up in workplace settings out of concern for how they may be perceived.

For many queer employees, workplace actions don’t match the visibility. That’s where tokenism becomes important. Tokenism is often misunderstood as simply having one LGBTQ+ employee on a team. 

In reality, workplace tokenism occurs when organizations prioritize visible demonstrations of diversity over meaningful systems of support. Instead of feeling truly seen and supported, it can lead queer employees to feel put on display. A company may feature queer employees in Pride Month campaigns, invite them to participate in inclusion initiatives, or publicly celebrate LGBTQ+ identities while failing to address concerns around discrimination, advancement opportunities, workplace culture, or employee protections.

Inclusion is about whether employees feel safe when something goes wrong, not about who appears in the Pride Month campaign.

Jennifer Brown, diversity and inclusion expert, said, “Diversity is being asked to the dance, and inclusion is being asked to dance.” During Pride Month, many companies extend the invitation. Companies feature LGBTQ+ employees in campaigns, invite them onto panels, and encourage them to share their stories, but being invited into the room is only the beginning. If employees don’t feel safe speaking up once they’re there, the celebration can start to feel less like inclusion and more like optics.

When Visibility Becomes A Substitute For Support

Pride Month visibility is not inherently a bad thing. Representation matters. Seeing LGBTQ+ identities acknowledged in the workplace can be validating, especially for employees who have spent years feeling invisible. But it’s not enough. Visibility cannot be the entire strategy. In order to truly do something, companies need to put systems in place to support their queer employees. 

Workplace inclusion strategist Lily Zheng wrote, “Workplace inclusion efforts ought to spend 40% of their time talking about inequality and 60% of their time fixing it. Too many spend 100% of their time talking, and in doing so, undermine their own legitimacy.”

Rainbow logos, social media posts, and once-a-year Pride events are not substitutes for genuinely felt inclusion.

That distinction is important because employees notice the gap between what organizations say and what they do. A company celebrating Pride publicly may offer limited LGBTQ-inclusive benefits, lack clear systems for reporting discrimination, and push for representation, all the while maintaining an overwhelmingly homogeneous leadership.

When organizations focus more on appearing inclusive than becoming inclusive, queer employees are not being put at the center of the initiative; the company’s image is. 

Why Tokenism Can Make Speaking Up More Difficult

At first glance, visibility might seem like progress. But tokenism can sometimes create a false sense of safety.

Employees see Pride campaigns, statements of support, and public commitments to inclusion and reasonably assume those values extend throughout the workplace. They may feel more comfortable raising concerns about exclusion, bias, or inequitable treatment.

When those concerns are dismissed, however, it can cause further marginalization. That’s because retaliation does not always look dramatic. Sometimes it looks like being labeled “difficult.” Sometimes it looks like being left out of opportunities, excluded from conversations, overlooked for advancement, or quietly discouraged from raising concerns again.

As diversity and inclusion expert Jennifer Brown has frequently emphasized throughout her work, diversity is about representation while inclusion is about belonging. One can exist without the other, but sustainable workplace cultures require both.

Article continues below advertisement

What Meaningful Inclusion Looks Like Beyond June

Meaningful inclusion is about walking the walk. For leadership, it looks like clear anti-discrimination and anti-retaliation policies. For employees, it looks like being an ally: speaking up on behalf of fellow employees. It’s about having LGBTQ-inclusive healthcare benefits, leadership accountability, employee resource groups, equitable promotion practices, and managers who know how to respond when concerns are raised.

More than anything, it’s about consistency. You can’t change an entire organization’s culture in a month, but you can put in place lasting practices that promote inclusion evolution over time. And yes, it can start during Pride Month, but it needs to last beyond that. True inclusion is not a campaign. It is a culture. Companies build culture by making employees feel respected, protected, and empowered every day of the year—not by deciding who appears in a Pride Month campaign.
